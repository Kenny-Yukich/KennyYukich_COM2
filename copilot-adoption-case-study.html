Copilot adoption at KEITH Manufacturing

Two licenses to 77, and 88% of them still active seven months later, at a walking-floor trailer manufacturer where most of the workforce doesn't sit at a desk.

Role
Sole builder and program owner
Timeframe
20 January 2026 to present, 31 weeks and running
Org
KEITH Manufacturing Co., ~200 people, Madras, Oregon
Stack
Microsoft 365 Copilot, Copilot Studio, Power Automate, SharePoint, Excel and PowerShell reporting
Where it stands
77 licensed users · 88% active on a rolling 28 days · reported weekly

The problem

Before this role existed, KEITH had two Microsoft 365 Copilot licenses. The CEO created the AI Productivity Coordinator position, we licensed the organization properly, and I started on 20 January 2026 with a real budget line and no adoption to show for it.

That's the moment most AI programs quietly fail. Licenses land in everyone's tenant, a few people try the thing, it doesn't obviously beat the way they already work, and within a month you're paying for software nobody opens. Nothing about buying the licenses solves that. The buying is the easy part.

Constraints

  • Most of the workforce is on the shop floor, not at a desk. Training time is measured in minutes, not hours.
  • No consultants, no dedicated team. One person, inside a wider IT role.
  • Anything requiring a new app or a new login was going to lose. Everything had to ship into Teams and M365, where people already were.
  • A visible spend with executive attention on it, which meant the program needed a number leadership could check without asking me.

What I built

Five pieces, all of them public now in the adoption kit:

  • A 90-day playbook. The rollout sequence, phase by phase, so the program didn't depend on me remembering what came next.
  • A role-specific prompt library. Prompts written against the work people at KEITH actually do, not generic examples.
  • Training built on their own tasks. Short, practical, no AI vocabulary.
  • A measurement loop. Covered below. This is the part most rollouts skip.
  • A governance checklist. What IT signed off on before anything went wide.

How the measurement works

I report every week. Week 31 went out in late August. The report is the program: without it, "adoption" is a feeling, and a feeling is not something you can act on before it's too late.

Four numbers, each answering a different question:

  • Rolling 28-day adoption. How many licensed users touched Copilot in the last four weeks. This is the breadth number, and the one leadership watches.
  • A 7-day active tracker in Excel. Breadth again, but tight enough to catch a slide while it's still fixable. My own usage is excluded, because counting the program owner flatters the number.
  • Total prompt volume. Depth across the org.
  • Average prompts per active user. Depth per person. This is the one that tells you whether people are actually working in the tool or just opening it.

There's a floor of 80% on the 28-day rate. A floor turns a soft goal into a threshold somebody has to explain, including me.

Where it stands at week 31

The rolling 28-day adoption rate is 88%, or 68 of 77 licensed users. Flat on the week and eight points clear of the floor. Seven months after rollout, not seven days.

The 7-day tracker sits at 68.4%, 52 of 76 licensed users excluding me, up from 67.1% the week before. Roughly two thirds of the company uses Copilot in any given week.

What's slipping, and what I'm doing about it

Breadth is holding. Depth is not. Prompt volume fell to 817 from 886, and average prompts per active user dropped to 15.7, the lowest reading since spring.

That pattern matters more than the headline percentage. It means people are still logging in but leaning on the tool less, which is what the front edge of churn looks like. An 88% adoption rate with a falling prompt count is a number that looks healthy right up until it isn't.

My read is that the initial training carried people to a set of tasks they now do on autopilot, and nothing has pushed them past it. The next phase is depth rather than reach: moving Copilot out of the chat box and into the work itself. I'm shipping agents that live inside an existing workflow in Teams, like part-number lookup for the support team and permission-aware certification lookups for safety, so using Copilot becomes how a task gets done rather than an extra thing to remember. The weekly report now carries a depth floor next to the breadth floor, so a falling prompt count in a department triggers a second training pass there instead of a shrug.

What I'd do differently

I front-loaded the training. One session per group carried people to a plateau, and I had no second session ready when they got there. I tracked breadth from day one and depth later, so I saw the plateau weeks late. And I wrote the prompt library before knowing which roles would use it; the prompts people use today are the ones they asked for.
